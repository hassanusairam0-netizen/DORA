D — DNA

  • DNA stands for Deoxyribonucleic Acid.
  • It carries the genetic instructions that help living things grow and function.
  • It holds information inherited from our parents.
  • It is shaped like a twisted ladder called a double helix.
  • In eukaryotic cells, most DNA is found in the nucleus.

Key idea

DNA is the instruction manual of a living organism.

DNA stands for deoxyribonucleic acid. It is like an instruction manual that tells living things how to grow and work. We inherit our DNA from our parents, which is why we can look like them. Point to the picture: DNA is shaped like a twisted ladder, called a double helix. The rungs of the ladder are pairs of bases. In cells like ours, most DNA is kept safely inside the nucleus.

O — Organelles

  • Organelles are specialized structures inside cells.
  • Each organelle has a particular job.
  • The nucleus contains most of the cell’s DNA.
  • Mitochondria release energy from food through cellular respiration.
  • The cell membrane controls what enters and leaves the cell.

Key idea

Organelles work together to keep a cell alive.

Organelles are like the tiny organs of a cell. Each one has its own job. Point to the nucleus: it holds most of the cell’s DNA, like a control centre. Point to the mitochondria: they release energy from food through cellular respiration. Point to the cell membrane: it is the outer border that controls what goes in and out. The jelly-like cytoplasm fills the space in between. All the organelles work together to keep the cell alive.

R — Ribosomes

  • Ribosomes are tiny structures found in cells.
  • They make proteins.
  • Proteins help cells grow, repair themselves and carry out important jobs.
  • Ribosomes float in the cytoplasm or are attached to the rough endoplasmic reticulum.
  • They are much smaller than most other cell structures.

Key idea

Ribosomes are the cell’s protein-making factories.

Ribosomes are tiny structures that build proteins. Point to the picture: the ribosome reads a copy of the instructions from DNA, called mRNA. It joins small building blocks called amino acids into a long chain. That chain folds up to become a protein. Proteins help cells grow, repair themselves and do important jobs. Some ribosomes float freely in the cytoplasm, and others are attached to the rough endoplasmic reticulum, or rough ER.

Key idea

Cells are tiny, but they contain many structures with different sizes and jobs.

Cells are far too small to see with our eyes, so scientists use tiny units. A micrometre is one-millionth of a metre, and a nanometre is one-billionth of a metre. One micrometre equals 1,000 nanometres. Read the table from top to bottom: each structure gets smaller. A typical animal cell is 10 to 30 micrometres, but a ribosome is only 20 to 30 nanometres, and the DNA helix is only about 2 nanometres wide.
